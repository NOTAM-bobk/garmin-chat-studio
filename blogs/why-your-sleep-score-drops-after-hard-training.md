You finish a long ride, sleep eight hours, and wake up to a sleep score the watch calls poor. Nothing about the night felt bad. Hard training does that, and the reason is mostly physiological rather than a tracking error.

## What a hard session does to the night

Training creates a repair job that runs for hours after the workout ends:

- **Core temperature stays elevated.** Cooling takes time, and sleep onset happens when temperature is falling. An evening session pushes that curve later.
- **Sympathetic tone stays high.** Heart rate and stress hormones take a while to settle, which shows up as higher overnight stress and a higher resting heart rate in the first part of the night.
- **Growth hormone and repair demand rise.** Deep sleep is where a lot of that work happens, so the body wants to prioritise it — but a late, intense session can fragment the first sleep cycles instead.
- **Fuelling and hydration shift.** A big deficit, or a very late meal, adds its own disruption.

Garmin's sleep score combines duration, stress during sleep, sleep stages, awake time, restlessness and blood oxygen. Three of those can move after a hard day even if you spend eight hours in bed.

## Reading the overnight numbers

Look at where the score is losing points rather than the total:

- **Deep sleep down, awake time up** — the classic post-session pattern. Usually one night.
- **Overnight stress high with normal sleep stages** — often a late session, alcohol, or a very hard effort close to bed.
- **Duration short with normal stages** — that is sleep debt, not training.
- **Blood oxygen dips** — worth watching across several nights, and worth raising with a clinician if it repeats.

The score is an average of things you can act on. A single bad night is information about last night, not a verdict on the block.

## Making the next night better

These are the levers that actually move the next score:

1. **Finish hard sessions at least three hours before bed.** This is the biggest single lever for most people.
2. **Cool down and cool the room.** A shower and a bedroom around 18°C helps the temperature drop that starts sleep.
3. **Eat enough, early enough.** A hard session plus a big deficit produces a restless night.
4. **Keep the wake time fixed.** Sleeping in after a bad night makes the following night harder.

Do not stack a second hard day on a poor night just to "get the session in". The session is cheaper to move than the recovery.

## When the pattern is not training

If sleep scores stay suppressed for a week while training is normal, the cause is usually outside the plan: a stressful stretch at work, travel, illness, alcohol, or a room that is too warm. In that case, adding rest days will not fix it and the readiness score will keep drifting down.

Check the trend across a week, not the number on a single morning, and treat a cluster of poor nights as a reason to look at load and life together.

Training guidance, not medical advice. Repeated breathing pauses or very low overnight oxygen readings deserve a proper conversation with a clinician.
