Training load is the least intuitive number in Garmin Connect and the one that best explains why a good week can still end in a flat one. Acute load and chronic load are not rival metrics; they are the same training seen over two windows.

## The two windows

**Acute load** is a weighted summary of roughly the last seven days. It rises quickly after a hard block and falls quickly when you rest.

**Chronic load** is the same calculation over roughly four weeks. It moves slowly, because it is the average of everything you have been doing.

Garmin then compares them. That comparison is your load ratio, and it answers a simple question: is this week bigger than what my body has been trained for?

## The ratio, without the mythology

The 0.8–1.3 band is a rough, widely used heuristic, not a law:

- **0.8–1.3** — sustainable. You can hold this for weeks.
- **1.3–1.5** — a deliberate overload. Fine for a short block, usually with an easier week after.
- **Above 1.5** — a spike. Sometimes planned (a race, a camp), often not.
- **Below 0.8** — a taper, an illness, or detraining. Context decides which.

The ratio is most useful as a rate-of-change warning. Going from 300 to 450 in a week is a much bigger ask than staying at 450 for a month.

## Load focus is the other half

Garmin also splits load by intensity: low aerobic, high aerobic and anaerobic. A big total built almost entirely from hard sessions is a different risk than the same total built from easy volume.

If your load focus is heavily anaerobic and your easy volume is thin, the ratio can look reasonable while the training is not. Most endurance athletes do better with a wide aerobic base and a small, sharp top end.

## What the numbers cannot see

Load is a calculation from heart rate and duration, so it does not know about:

- the surface you ran on, or a niggle that changed your stride;
- the heat, or a session done at altitude;
- sleep debt from a bad week;
- strength work that never reached the watch.

That is why load should be read next to sleep, HRV and resting heart rate. A load spike with stable HRV and normal sleep is usually fine. The same spike with three poor nights is a warning.

## A practical weekly routine

1. Check load ratio once a week, not daily.
2. Keep the increase gradual: roughly a tenth more than your four-week average per week.
3. Plan an easier week every third or fourth week rather than waiting for the ratio to force one.
4. When the ratio spikes unexpectedly, look for the session that did it and decide whether it earned its place.

The point of tracking load is not to hit a target number. It is to notice when this week quietly became much bigger than the last month — before your body does.

Training guidance, not medical advice.
