Heart rate variability is the most over-interpreted number on a training watch. It is genuinely useful, but only when you read it the way the watch intends: as a slow signal against your own baseline, not a daily score to chase.

## What HRV actually measures

HRV is the variation in time between heartbeats. A higher value generally means the parasympathetic branch of the nervous system has more influence — broadly, that you are more recovered. A lower value suggests the sympathetic branch is still dominant.

That relationship is real but loose. HRV responds to training, sleep, alcohol, illness, hydration, altitude and stress. It also simply varies. Two nights with identical behaviour can produce meaningfully different readings.

## How Garmin builds the baseline

Garmin measures overnight HRV during sleep and compares a rolling seven-night average to a personal baseline built over roughly three weeks. That comparison produces the status: balanced, unbalanced, low or high.

Three practical consequences:

- **A new watch or a break from wearing it overnight resets the picture.** Ignore the status until the baseline is ready.
- **One night cannot change the status much.** That is deliberate.
- **Your baseline is personal.** A "low" value for you may be perfectly normal for someone else.

## What a real change looks like

The signal worth acting on is a cluster, not a night:

| Pattern | Read |
| --- | --- |
| One night below baseline, normal resting HR | Noise. Ignore. |
| Three to five nights below baseline | Recovery is lagging. Reduce intensity. |
| Below baseline plus resting HR up 5+ bpm | Strong sign to back off and protect sleep. |
| Above baseline for weeks with flat training | Often good recovery; occasionally a sign of under-loading. |
| Status "low" for a week or more | Check illness, sleep debt and life stress first. |

If you want a simple rule: act when two independent signals agree. HRV below baseline plus a rising resting heart rate is far more convincing than either alone.

## The mistakes that make HRV useless

- **Comparing daily values.** The day-to-day noise swamps any real change.
- **Reacting to a single low night.** The classic overreaction is cancelling a good session after one poor reading.
- **Chasing a higher number.** HRV is not a score to maximise, and trying to raise it usually means training less than you should.
- **Ignoring context.** A low reading after a late night, a flight or two glasses of wine says more about last night than about your fitness.

## How to use it in practice

Check the seven-night trend and the status once a morning. If the status is balanced, train as planned. If it is unbalanced with a normal resting heart rate, keep the session but drop the intensity. If it is low and resting heart rate is also up, make the hard session easy and get an early night.

Then get on with the week. HRV is a background instrument, not a steering wheel.
