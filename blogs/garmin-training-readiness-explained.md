Training readiness is the one Garmin number people check before deciding whether to train hard. It is also the one most often misread. It is not a score of how fit you are, and it is not a verdict on today's session. It is a guess about how much strain you can absorb right now, built from a handful of signals that all update overnight.

Here is what is inside it, what moves it, and how to use it without turning every dip into a rest day.

## The inputs behind the score

Garmin builds readiness from six things:

- **Sleep score** — total sleep, sleep stages, restlessness and overnight stress.
- **Recovery time** — the hours Garmin estimates until you can absorb another hard effort.
- **HRV status** — your overnight heart-rate variability against your own baseline.
- **Acute load** — the training load you accumulated over the last week.
- **Sleep history** — the last few nights, not just last night.
- **Stress history** — daytime stress from the previous days.

Most of the movement you see day to day comes from sleep and acute load. A brutal session raises acute load for days, so readiness can stay suppressed even after you feel fine. That lag is the score working as intended, not a bug.

## What a number actually tells you

Garmin bands the score from very low to high. The band matters less than the trend:

| Pattern | What it usually means |
| --- | --- |
| High for weeks, then a two-day slide | A real load spike. Back off one session. |
| Low every Monday | Weekend load, not a health problem. |
| Low with flat HRV and normal resting HR | Sleep debt or life stress, not overtraining. |
| Low with HRV below baseline and resting HR up | Recovery is genuinely lagging. Cut intensity. |

A single low morning after a late night is noise. Three low mornings with HRV below baseline is a trend worth acting on.

## How to act on it

Use readiness to choose the shape of the session, not whether you train at all:

1. **High** — run the hard session you planned. Quality work belongs here.
2. **Moderate** — keep the session, trim the volume by about a fifth, and keep the intervals honest.
3. **Low** — easy aerobic work only, and check the other signals before you decide.
4. **Very low for several days** — take the easy day, look at sleep and load together, and only then consider a lighter week.

The score is genuinely useful when you pair it with the data underneath. A low readiness number with normal HRV and a resting heart rate at baseline usually means sleep debt, not a training problem.

## Where it goes wrong

Readiness punishes hard training by design. If you race or do a long effort, expect a suppressed score for two or three days and do not chase it back to green. It also cannot see the things that matter most: a stressful week at work, a cold coming on, or a bad night with a child awake. Treat the number as one input, not the input.

## The useful habit

Check readiness once, in the morning, and write down the one reason it moved. After a month you will know your own pattern — which sessions cost you two days, how much sleep debt drops the score, and whether your HRV responds before or after you feel it. That pattern is worth more than any single morning's number.

Training guidance, not medical advice. If a low score comes with unusual resting heart rate, illness or chest symptoms, talk to a clinician.
